Cockpit Preparation - Flow Memorization Plan

How to commit the Cockpit Preparation flow to memory as ten colored groups, and how to practise it so it survives the thing that actually breaks it. Source of truth: data/phase_flows.json (A330P FCOM R17 PRO-NOR-SOP-03 / 04 / 06, audited against the FCOM 22 Sep 2026). Method is sourced; where a technique is one pilot's opinion rather than documented practice, it says so.

Target: Cockpit Preparation, 46 card items in 10 groups, CM2 drilling 29 as PF and 35 as PM. The groups, their counts and their item names on this page are generated from the phase flows; the trainer drills the same data.

0. What you are actually up against

NASA Ames ran a line observation study across 20 flight segments focused on the preflight phase. Two numbers from it set the stakes:

  • 23 percent of errors and 38 percent of threats occur before the aircraft moves.
  • The preflight in that study was 50+ flow items followed by a 13-item checklist. Yours is larger.

The mechanism they identified is the one that will get you: crews must interleave novel, unpredictable tasks (paperwork, weather, a gate agent at your shoulder) with a well-practiced habitual sequence. The habit is what makes the interruption dangerous, because a sequence running on autopilot does not consciously encode where it stopped.

So the goal is not "know the flow." The goal is know the flow, survive being stopped in the middle of it, and be able to run it from either seat.

2. Why ten, and why these

Four gates that read as verbs (OFF, ON, SET UP, START) carry the whole of preliminary prep for CM2 with nothing to count; the verb tells you the state of every switch in the group. The seats split at BOX START: from there the PF works the panels and the box while the PM clears the aircraft to walk. Two short check groups close each branch (2 for PF, 3 for PM), and the flow ends in a triple every crew already says out loud: legs, brief, checklist. Ten names, in one physical direction through the cockpit, is inside the four-to-seven item span working memory can hold, and each name regenerates its own items.

3. The groups, item by item, with the hook for each

CM2 · TURN IT OFF · 4

Four things that must be dead before anything else: engines, radar, gear lever position, wipers. Say the four nouns; the state is always OFF, DOWN, NORM.

  1. ENG masters OFF · ENG START NORM
  2. Weather radar OFF *
  3. L/G lever DOWN
  4. Wipers OFF

CM2 · TURN IT ON · 6

Power, talk, fire, air, nav, light, weather: batteries and external power, radios, the APU fire test, APU start and bleed, IRS to NAV, cockpit lights, ATIS. Seven, in the order the airplane comes alive.

  1. Batteries check / ELEC
  2. RMP 1 and 2 CHECK ON
  3. APU FIRE pb-sw
  4. APU start · bleed
  5. ADIRS * ALL IR MODE selector NAV
  6. Cockpit lights * AS RQRD

CM2 · SET IT UP · 5

The paperwork gate. MCDU pre-init and RCL and acceptance are CM1; EFB, logbook and MEL, OEB, Jeppesen are everyone. Four for CM2.

  8. Jeppesen charts * PREPARE

The PF opens the box: ACARS, then the preliminary takeoff numbers. Two items and the seats split here.

4. The practice protocol

This is the part that matters. The structures above are one evening of work. The protocol is three weeks.

Phase 1, days 1 to 3 - do-list, seated, card in hand

Run the flow reading the card, touching every switch. Slow. No attempt to recall. You are building the motor path and the panel geography, not memory.

Hard rule from AOPA's chair-flying guidance: do not rehearse a botched rep. If you fumble a sequence, stop, re-read it, and start the block again. You are strengthening whatever you rehearse, including the errors.

Phase 2, days 4 to 10 - flow first, card second

This ordering is not optional and it is not stylistic. Flight Safety Foundation ALAR states it directly: normal checklists are not read-and-do lists, and are accomplished after performing the flow defined in the SOPs.

The reason is in Degani and Wiener's NASA study. Challenge-response preserves what they call configuration redundancy and mutual redundancy: you configure from memory, then a second independent pass verifies. A do-list collapses the two into one pass, and a mistake passes unnoticed once the sequence is interrupted.

If you learn this flow by reading the checklist, you will have built a do-list habit and destroyed the redundancy the checklist exists to provide.

So: run the block cold from the gate name or station name. Then pick up the card and verify. Never the reverse.

Phase 3, days 8 onward, overlapping - randomize and interrupt

Two countermeasures against documented failure modes. These are the two things almost nobody does, and they are the two that decide whether this holds up.

Randomize the panel before every rep. Degani and Wiener observed expectation bias directly on the line: the flap handle sits at zero, the pilot perceives it at 5 because he expects it there, and calls "flaps 5." Their verdict on how that forms: the routine which developed turned into a very tough habit to break. A garage cockpit where every switch is always in the same place trains exactly that error. Before each rep, have someone set switches wrong, or set them wrong yourself the night before and run it cold in the morning.

Every rep must terminate in a reported state, not a remembered action. Not "bat one, AUTO." Rather: "bat one, I am looking at it, AUTO."

Get interrupted on purpose. The NASA preflight study splits disruptions into uncertainties (fuel, lighting, IRS alignment: these force rescheduling) and intrusions (cabin crew, gate agents, ground: these demand immediate attention). Practicing clean does not train the thing that fails.

Have Elaine or a study partner interrupt you at a random point. Score two things only:

  • Did you verbalize an explicit hold before your attention left the flow?
  • On resumption, did you go back one item, not forward?

Both come from FSF ALAR Briefing Note 2.4 and the SKYbrary OGHFA note. The recovery frame is Identify, Ask, Decide: what was I doing, where was I interrupted, what action gets me back on track.

Worth knowing: Degani and Wiener found that although some airlines mandate a hold callout, most pilots interviewed said they rarely actually use it. The defense exists on paper and not in practice. Building it into your reps now is free.

Phase 3b - drill the branch, not the duplicate

The two role versions of preliminary prep share about 93 percent of their content. A PF day followed by a PM day therefore rehearses the spine twice and the branch point never, and the branch point is the only place an error can live. Concentrate the reps there instead.

The rep: a partner calls your role at random as you sit down, not you. You run the fifteen at full working pace with no pauses, since the flows run simultaneously here and you are not required to watch the other seat. Then score exactly one binary: did the P fire when it should have, and stay dead when it should not have. Occasionally have them swap your role on you mid-flow, which forces a deliberate re-index rather than autopilot.

The failure mode is negative transfer, and it runs one direction: as PM you will reach for ACARS, because most of your recent reps ended there. The tell is your hand moving before you have said your role out loud. So start every rep with three words, out loud, before your hand leaves your lap: CM2, PM. Or CM2, PF.

Phase 4, days 11 to 21 - role swap and stress

Alternate PF and PM cuts day by day. Never two days in the same role.

There is no documented technique for keeping role versions separate. The only method that surfaced, from a type-rating account, is observational: you learn the other role by watching it, not by running it. Practically, that means when you chair-fly with Win, one of you runs it and the other watches and scores rather than both running silently.

Then run it tired, run it after a leg, run it in the garage with the radio on. The quiet-room rep is the easy rep.

5. Documented failure modes to score yourself against

All observed on the line by Degani and Wiener, not speculation.

FailureWhat it looks like
Touch without readHand goes to the checklist, card never comes out of the slot
Verification collapseChallenge read without eyes leaving the card; response given before looking
ChunkingSeveral challenge items called together, chunked response back
Expectation biasItem perceived where it should be, not where it is
Sing-songFlow run, replies from memory, not from the actual state
Layout mismatchChecklist order fights panel geometry, forcing a non-intuitive path

Score a rep as failed if any of these show up, even if every item was named.

6. The exception rack

Drill these separately from the flow. They are what the evaluator reaches for. Every line below is carried in data/phase_flows.json against its FCOM ident.

  • Battery voltage above 25.5 V if the aircraft has been unpowered 6 h or more
  • No APU bleed with a ground air unit connected
  • Aircraft acceptance may be performed later, but must be complete by the end of Cockpit Preparation
  • Flaps: check the position agrees with the handle. If surfaces disagree with handles, check with maintenance before applying hydraulic power
  • ACCU PRESS must sit in the green band; recharge with the blue electric pump if it does not
  • Park brake: avoid applying if one brake is above 300 °C (150 °C with brake fans ON) [FCOM PRO-NOR-SOP-07 / SOP-22], unless operationally necessary
  • OXY half boxed amber: go to the MIN FLT CREW OXY CHART, do not eyeball it
  • Alternate braking check, first flight of the day: chocks in place, PARK BRK OFF, pedals press, 2 200 to 2 700 PSI at full deflection and symmetric, pedals release, PARK BRK ON
  • IRS align check at Cockpit Prep: POSITION MONITOR page, IRS in NAV, each IRS within 5 NM of FMS position
  • Three gear pins onboard and stowed
  • Warn ground crew on intercom before the oxygen mask test
  • STS pb: INOP SYS must be compatible with the MEL

8. Open questions to resolve with your own training department

  • RESOLVED: flows run simultaneously at Alaska/Hawaiian. You are not required to watch the other pilot's flow. So practise at full working pace with no pauses waiting on the other seat, and treat verification as happening at the defined crosscheck points (FMS PREPARATION CROSSCHECK, departure legs verification, the checklist), not continuously.
  • RESOLVED: the PF/PM split in preliminary prep is two items, not two flows. Verified against data/phase_flows.json: the three CM2 groups carry 15 card items, and PF · BOX START (ACARS INITIALIZE, PRELIMINARY TAKEOFF PERF DATA) is the only PF-tagged pair before the branch.
  • RESOLVED: the full-alignment condition list (first flight of the day, crew change, departure between 2° N and 2° S, GPS not available with poor NAVAID coverage or flight time more than 3 h) is now carried on the ADIRS item in the phase flows, quoted from FCOM PRO-NOR-SOP-04-00011860.
  • Confirm the A in A-DIFSRIPP against your printed card. The card does not expand it.
  • Pick and record your overhead scan direction, since no source prescribes one.
