Cockpit Preparation - Flow Memorization Plan

How to commit the three biggest ground flows to memory, and how to practise them so they survive the thing that actually breaks them. Method is sourced; where a technique is one pilot's opinion rather than documented practice, it says so.

Target flows: Preliminary Cockpit Prep (22 card lines, of which the CM2 column is 14 numbered items plus the EFB, tagged ALL), Before Walkaround (10, every one PM), Cockpit Preparation (16 card lines over ~60 FCOM document units). Source of truth: data/flows.json, A330P FCOM R17 PRO-NOR-SOP-04/05/06, A330P NPC-CB 2025-08-13 Cockpit Preparation card, FCTM PR-NP-CL.

Evidence basis for the method is cited inline. Where something is one pilot's opinion rather than documented practice, it says so.

0. What you are actually up against

NASA Ames ran a line observation study across 20 flight segments focused on the preflight phase. Two numbers from it set the stakes:

  • 23 percent of errors and 38 percent of threats occur before the aircraft moves.
  • The preflight in that study was 50+ flow items followed by a 13-item checklist. Yours is larger.

The mechanism they identified is the one that will get you: crews must interleave novel, unpredictable tasks (paperwork, weather, a gate agent at your shoulder) with a well-practiced habitual sequence. The habit is what makes the interruption dangerous, because a sequence running on autopilot does not consciously encode where it stopped.

So the goal is not "know the flow." The goal is know the flow, survive being stopped in the middle of it, and be able to run it from either seat.

1. Four layers, in this order

Do not try to hold 48 items. Hold four structures, and let each one generate its own items.

Plus two satellite structures: A-DIFSRIPP for the FMS, and the exception rack.

1b. The ten colored groups (21 Sep 2026, current)

This is the version the Flows Trainer and the Phase Flows page now drill. The gates, the two questions and the spiral below are the reasoning; the ten groups are what you say out loud. Each group has a color, a name and a count, and the count is the CM2 count. Say the name, then the count, then run the items. Role only ever adds: CM2 as PF runs 29 items, CM2 as PM runs 35, and the spine (Turn it OFF, Turn it ON, Set it up, Box build and check, Legs Brief Checklist) is identical in both.

One deliberate departure from the printed card: the card numbers ATIS as item 13, after the logbook and before the Jeppesen charts. Turn it ON closes with ATIS as its seventh item so the radios block ends with the listen, and Set it up is then four things you open rather than switch. Everything else in the ten groups runs in card order.

Order of the groups is the order of the day: the four spine groups of preliminary prep, then the PF fork (Box start), then Cockpit Preparation opens with the second fork (PF Panels, PM Before Walkaround), rejoins for Box build and check, forks a third time for the checks (PF 2, PM 3), and closes together on Legs, Brief, Checklist. Three forks, one spine. The color is the retrieval cue: if you cannot remember the next item, name the color you are standing in and the count tells you whether you are done.

2. Layer one - the gates (the reasoning behind Turn it OFF / Turn it ON / Set it up / Box start)

Revised 19 Sep 2026. The gates below run in card order. The earlier version of this page grouped by category and put RMP and ATIS together in ALIGN, which reads well and executes wrong: RMP is card item 6, between the batteries and the APU fire test, and ATIS is card item 13, after the logbook. Running the old gates in gate order meant running the card out of order. RMP has moved into POWER and ATIS into LEAP's acceptance bundle. ALIGN is now one item. Podcast Ep5 and Ep6 still teach the old grouping; Ep7 teaches this one.

Two things to hold before the gates: your column is fifteen items whether or not you have the leg, and in this flow your role never subtracts, it only adds.

SAFE (4, CM2)

Nothing starts, nothing radiates, nothing retracts, nothing wipes. ENG 1, 2 MASTERS LEVERS OFF. WXR/PWS sw OFF. L/G lever DOWN. Both WIPER selectors OFF. The same DUs also carry ENG START selector NORM, and on the radar DU the CAPT and F/O DISPLAY mode selectors OFF, GAIN AUTO, TURB AUTO, none of which the card prints.

POWER (5, CM2)

Cards 5 through 9, and the order is causal rather than arbitrary. BAT 1, BAT 2 and APU BAT pb-sw AUTO, plus EXT PWR pb ON when the AVAIL light is on. Then RMP 1 and 2 CHECK ON, green NAV light off, frequencies tuned: VHF 1 ATC, VHF 2 ATIS and company, VHF 3 ACARS. Then APU FIRE pb-sw IN and GUARDED with the agent light OFF, then APU FIRE TEST pb PRESS and MAINTAIN. Then APU MASTER SW pb-sw ON, APU START pb-sw ON. Then APU BLEED pb-sw ON when AVAIL, X-BLEED rotary AUTO, cockpit and cabin temp as required.

RMP sits here because the card puts it here, and the card puts it here because power is what makes radios possible. Batteries, then radios, then the APU, then air. You cannot run this gate out of order without violating the causal chain, which is what makes it stick.

ALIGN (1, CM2)

ALL IR MODE selector NAV. One item, and it earns its own gate: it is the only thing in preliminary prep with a clock running on it. Everything else is a switch that answers instantly. This one is a process that takes minutes and does not care about your schedule, so it gets its own name and its own moment.

LEAP (4, CM2, plus the fork)

Lights. EFB. Acceptance. Performance.

L, card 11: COCKPIT LIGHTS AS RQRD, including INT, FLOOD and INTEG on the glareshield and FCU. E: EFB START, tagged ALL so both seats every time, then EFB/eQRH version check and the EFB status page. A, cards 12 to 14: LOGBOOK AND MEL/CDL ITEMS CHECK plus the aircraft configuration summary and OEBs (all tagged ALL; the AIRCRAFT ACCEPTANCE PERFORM line itself is CM1's), then ATIS OBTAIN, then JEPPESEN CHARTS PREPARE with routing and ETPs in Jepp FD Pro. FCOM allows acceptance to be performed later provided it is complete by the end of Cockpit Preparation.

P is the fork. Performance is the only role-indexed part of this flow, and it is PF only: ACARS INITIALIZE (departure, destination, flight number, date) and PRELIMINARY TAKEOFF PERF DATA OBTAIN off the AeroData TPR or TLR (runway, intersection, flap setting, anti-ice, packs, thrust option). As PM you stop after the charts.

LEAP as a mnemonic is not mine. It is a published Airbus operator mnemonic from the A320 Normal Procedures document at theairlinepilots.com. What is new here is that its last letter happens to land exactly on the role boundary.

The whole role question, in one line

Of the 22 card lines, 20 are indexed by SEAT and 2 by role, both PF, both in a small PF/PM sub-block under the main card. So: the only thing your role changes in preliminary prep is the last letter of LEAP. The second and larger fork is Before Walkaround, which is 100 percent PM, with an empty PF column. As CM2 you either add two items or add ten. You never drop any.

3. Layer two - Before Walkaround, two questions

All PM, and that is not a summary, it is the card: the PF column on this flow is empty. If you have the leg you do none of it. If you are monitoring you own all ten. Do not hold ten items. Hold two questions and sort.

Can it move? Flaps position. Speed brake retracted and disarmed. ACCU PRESS. PARK BRK ON. BRAKES PRESS. Chocks in place. Three gear pins onboard and stowed.

Can it hurt you? Oxygen pressure. Hydraulic reservoir levels. Engine oil quantity. Emergency equipment. Rain repellent pressure and quantity.

First three of the second bucket come off SD pages: DOOR/OXY, HYD, ENG. Two conditionals: chocks not in means park brake set with adequate accumulator pressure; gear door open means do not pressurize hydraulics until ground clears.

4. Layer three - the spiral (the reasoning behind Panels, Box build and check, the checks, and Legs Brief Checklist)

Airbus designs flows as closed continuous shapes. Their own vocabulary for the taxi flow is "U-shaped," and in the 2022 SOP revision they reversed that U to accommodate different weather radar installations and to standardize across the fleet for mixed-fleet flying. Flows follow the panel, not the pilot.

Consequence for you: do not invent your own order. Learn the shape the panel imposes, and never reverse it once learned.

Eight stations, same direction every time:

  • Overhead (PF) - all white lights OFF, umbrella over 14 DUs
  • Center instrument panel (PF) - ISIS, north ref, ECAM switching, clock, gravity extension OFF, A/SKID and N/W STRG ON
  • Pedestal (PF) - RMP, ACP, park brake, door test, switching panel, engines (levers IDLE, reversers STOWED, masters OFF, start NORM), ATC/TCAS STBY
  • MCDU (PF) - A-DIFSRIPP
  • Glareshield (BOTH) - baro set/crosscheck, loudspeaker, EFIS CP, FD, LS, ND mode and range, ADF/VOR, FCU speed/mach dashed, HDG-V/S, ALT to initial expected clearance altitude
  • Lateral consoles (BOTH) - oxygen mask test, then DOOR/OXY SD REGUL LO PR not displayed
  • PFD and ND (BOTH) - DMC selector NORM, brightness, PFD check, ND check
  • ECAM (PF) - PRESS pb, STS pb (INOP SYS compatible with MEL), FOB check

Closeout: IRS align check (PM), release/fitness/FOB/PDSC sent (PF), FOB (PM), ATC clearance confirm (PM), departure legs verification (BOTH), departure briefing (BOTH).

Departure Briefing complete triggers the Cockpit Preparation checklist.

On overhead scan direction specifically: there is no Airbus FCOM or FCTM statement prescribing one that I could find, and no operator mnemonic for the overhead exists. The only published convention is from FlyByWire Simulations (left to right, bottom to top, in three sub-blocks), which is a simulation community project, not an operator. Treat it as one credible convention. Pick a direction, write it down, and never change it. Consistency matters more than which direction you choose.

5. A-DIFSRIPP

Not a Hawaiian invention. It is the standard Airbus FMGS setup mnemonic used across A320 and A330 operators. Spoken "ay DIF-srip."

LetterPage
AATC Datalink - MSG ERASE, ADS CHECK ARMED
DDATA
IINIT A
FF-PLN
SSEC F-PLN
RRAD NAV
IINIT B
PPERF
PPROG

Two things to know that make it stick:

The S was added on purpose. The original was DIFRIP, and operators kept finding crews who skipped the secondary flight plan. The S was promoted ahead of RAD NAV to force it into the sequence. That letter is a scar. Knowing why a letter exists makes it survive stress in a way rote order does not.

INIT B sits at position six deliberately. An operator training document states it plainly: insert the weights after completing all other insertions, to avoid cycles of prediction computations. A legacy FMS concern that persists as good practice. If someone tells you to do INIT B early, that is why the mnemonic disagrees.

PM half is one word: crosscheck. Eight things entered, eight things verified: departure route waypoints and constraints, flight plan against CFP, initial cruise altitude, total track miles, fuel at destination, performance data, TRANS ALT, SEC flight plan.

6. Layer four - pairs

Six coordinated items. Half of a coordinated item is a miss. Encode who calls and who answers, both directions.

EFB initialization (ALL). Barometric reference set/crosscheck (BOTH). Oxygen mask test (PF-PM). PFD and ND check (PF-PM). Departure legs verification (BOTH, PF reads from ND in PLAN with CSTR on, PM verifies against flight plan, Jepp charts and clearance). Departure briefing (BOTH, threat based per FOM).

7. The practice protocol

This is the part that matters. The structures above are one evening of work. The protocol is three weeks.

Phase 1, days 1 to 3 - do-list, seated, card in hand

Run the flow reading the card, touching every switch. Slow. No attempt to recall. You are building the motor path and the panel geography, not memory.

Hard rule from AOPA's chair-flying guidance: do not rehearse a botched rep. If you fumble a sequence, stop, re-read it, and start the block again. You are strengthening whatever you rehearse, including the errors.

Phase 2, days 4 to 10 - flow first, card second

This ordering is not optional and it is not stylistic. Flight Safety Foundation ALAR states it directly: normal checklists are not read-and-do lists, and are accomplished after performing the flow defined in the SOPs.

The reason is in Degani and Wiener's NASA study. Challenge-response preserves what they call configuration redundancy and mutual redundancy: you configure from memory, then a second independent pass verifies. A do-list collapses the two into one pass, and a mistake passes unnoticed once the sequence is interrupted.

If you learn this flow by reading the checklist, you will have built a do-list habit and destroyed the redundancy the checklist exists to provide.

So: run the block cold from the gate name or station name. Then pick up the card and verify. Never the reverse.

Phase 3, days 8 onward, overlapping - randomize and interrupt

Two countermeasures against documented failure modes. These are the two things almost nobody does, and they are the two that decide whether this holds up.

Randomize the panel before every rep. Degani and Wiener observed expectation bias directly on the line: the flap handle sits at zero, the pilot perceives it at 5 because he expects it there, and calls "flaps 5." Their verdict on how that forms: the routine which developed turned into a very tough habit to break. A garage cockpit where every switch is always in the same place trains exactly that error. Before each rep, have someone set switches wrong, or set them wrong yourself the night before and run it cold in the morning.

Every rep must terminate in a reported state, not a remembered action. Not "bat one, AUTO." Rather: "bat one, I am looking at it, AUTO."

Get interrupted on purpose. The NASA preflight study splits disruptions into uncertainties (fuel, lighting, IRS alignment: these force rescheduling) and intrusions (cabin crew, gate agents, ground: these demand immediate attention). Practicing clean does not train the thing that fails.

Have Elaine or a study partner interrupt you at a random point. Score two things only:

  • Did you verbalize an explicit hold before your attention left the flow?
  • On resumption, did you go back one item, not forward?

Both come from FSF ALAR Briefing Note 2.4 and the SKYbrary OGHFA note. The recovery frame is Identify, Ask, Decide: what was I doing, where was I interrupted, what action gets me back on track.

Worth knowing: Degani and Wiener found that although some airlines mandate a hold callout, most pilots interviewed said they rarely actually use it. The defense exists on paper and not in practice. Building it into your reps now is free.

Phase 3b - drill the branch, not the duplicate

The two role versions of preliminary prep share about 93 percent of their content. A PF day followed by a PM day therefore rehearses the spine twice and the branch point never, and the branch point is the only place an error can live. Concentrate the reps there instead.

The rep: a partner calls your role at random as you sit down, not you. You run the fifteen at full working pace with no pauses, since the flows run simultaneously here and you are not required to watch the other seat. Then score exactly one binary: did the P fire when it should have, and stay dead when it should not have. Occasionally have them swap your role on you mid-flow, which forces a deliberate re-index rather than autopilot.

The failure mode is negative transfer, and it runs one direction: as PM you will reach for ACARS, because most of your recent reps ended there. The tell is your hand moving before you have said your role out loud. So start every rep with three words, out loud, before your hand leaves your lap: CM2, PM. Or CM2, PF.

Phase 4, days 11 to 21 - role swap and stress

Alternate PF and PM cuts day by day. Never two days in the same role.

There is no documented technique for keeping role versions separate. The only method that surfaced, from a type-rating account, is observational: you learn the other role by watching it, not by running it. Practically, that means when you chair-fly with Win, one of you runs it and the other watches and scores rather than both running silently.

Then run it tired, run it after a leg, run it in the garage with the radio on. The quiet-room rep is the easy rep.

8. Documented failure modes to score yourself against

All observed on the line by Degani and Wiener, not speculation.

FailureWhat it looks like
Touch without readHand goes to the checklist, card never comes out of the slot
Verification collapseChallenge read without eyes leaving the card; response given before looking
ChunkingSeveral challenge items called together, chunked response back
Expectation biasItem perceived where it should be, not where it is
Sing-songFlow run, replies from memory, not from the actual state
Layout mismatchChecklist order fights panel geometry, forcing a non-intuitive path

Score a rep as failed if any of these show up, even if every item was named.

9. The exception rack

Drill these separately from the flow. They are what the evaluator reaches for. Every line below is carried in data/flows.json against its FCOM ident.

  • Battery voltage above 25.5 V if the aircraft has been unpowered 6 h or more
  • No APU bleed with a ground air unit connected
  • Aircraft acceptance may be performed later, but must be complete by the end of Cockpit Preparation
  • Flaps: check the position agrees with the handle. If surfaces disagree with handles, check with maintenance before applying hydraulic power
  • ACCU PRESS must sit in the green band; recharge with the blue electric pump if it does not
  • Park brake: avoid applying if one brake is above 500 C, or 350 C with brake fans ON, unless operationally necessary
  • OXY half boxed amber: go to the MIN FLT CREW OXY CHART, do not eyeball it
  • Alternate braking check, first flight of the day: chocks in place, PARK BRK OFF, pedals press, 2 200 to 2 700 PSI at full deflection and symmetric, pedals release, PARK BRK ON
  • IRS align check at Cockpit Prep: POSITION MONITOR page, IRS in NAV, each IRS within 5 NM of FMS position
  • Three gear pins onboard and stowed
  • Warn ground crew on intercom before the oxygen mask test
  • STS pb: INOP SYS must be compatible with the MEL

11. Open questions to resolve with your own training department

  • RESOLVED: flows run simultaneously at Alaska/Hawaiian. You are not required to watch the other pilot's flow. So practise at full working pace with no pauses waiting on the other seat, and treat verification as happening at the defined crosscheck points (FMS PREPARATION CROSSCHECK, departure legs verification, the checklist), not continuously.
  • RESOLVED: the PF/PM split in preliminary prep is two items, not two flows. Verified against data/flows.json, CM2 column n1-14 plus EFB (ALL), with ACARS INITIALIZE and PRELIMINARY TAKEOFF PERF DATA the only PF-tagged lines.
  • OPEN: the condition list for a full IRS alignment rather than a fast one is not on this card and is not in the flow data. Pull it from the FCOM ADIRS section and record it here. Not quoted on this page because it has not been verified against the source.
  • Confirm the A in A-DIFSRIPP against your printed card. The card does not expand it.
  • Pick and record your overhead scan direction, since no source prescribes one.
